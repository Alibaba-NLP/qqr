This image displays a short horizontal handscroll in ink and colour on silk, depicting a formal diplomatic audience at the Tang imperial court, presented in stacked horizontal sections. It is the most famous surviving work of historical-event painting from the early Tang dynasty.

1. The Left Group (The Envoys)
Three standing figures wait on the left. The foremost is a dignified envoy from the Tibetan court: a bearded man in narrow-sleeved crimson robe with alternating red-and-black rounded panels, a black small-brimmed hat, and heavy boots, hands clasped before his chest. Two officials in loose court robes flank him — one in grey-white, one with a sash — waiting gravely.

2. The Right Group (The Emperor's Party)
At the centre-right, the emperor — a tall, calm figure in dark six-paneled cap and russet-yellow robe — sits on a litter (sedan chair) borne by nine palace ladies-in-waiting. The attendants, in striped red-and-black and pale robes, bend under the litter poles with fan-shaped and embroidered canopies (two large round fans with bird-and-flower medallions) raised above. One attendant fans, another steadies the litter; the women's small round faces and dark crescent eyebrows reflect Tang court taste.

3. Details
- Red square collector seals and columns of small-scale inscriptions appear at the top and along the margins — including the signatures of later Song and Yuan connoisseurs who examined the scroll.
- The ground is plain warm ochre-silk; a minimal suggestion of steps and a fringed canopy frame the scene.
- Colour is concentrated in the crimson robe and the yellow imperial garment; everything else is restrained ink and pale tints.

4. Style
Figures are drawn with fine, even iron-wire outlines; sizes are graded by rank (the emperor largest, the envoy next, attendants smallest). The composition — envoys left, court right — is severe, balanced and entirely portrait-like.

Overall Impression
The scroll commemorates the marriage-alliance embassy of 641 CE, when the Tibetan minister came to request a Tang princess for his king. As a document of state portraiture it is matchless: one of the earliest narrative handscrolls whose attribution to the early Tang master of court painting has never been seriously doubted.
