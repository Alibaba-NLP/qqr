This image displays a traditional Chinese ink landscape handscroll, executed in monochrome ink wash with subtle tonal gradations, presented in stacked horizontal sections. It is a classic literati "shanshui" composition celebrating the scenery of a famous river region south of the lower Yangtze.

1. The Inscription (Upper Right)
The upper right margin carries a long vertical colophon in running script. The opening line is dated to a jiawu year of the Zhizheng era ("至正甲午...") and ends with the signature of a Daoist painter of the Fuchun hills; several red collector seals accompany the text, and one seal near the colophon reads the four-character title of the work. The colophon records that the scroll was begun for a fellow Daoist named Wuyong and completed only after three or four years.

2. The Landscape
The composition follows the "near, middle, far" convention.
- The Left Side: A broad expanse of water with faint, atmospheric ink-wash mountains beyond; a small fishing boat rides near the shore.
- The Center: Flat riverbanks with sparse trees, scattered cottages, a thatched pavilion and low hills; brushwork alternates dry, wrinkled texture strokes (cun) with moist washes.
- The Right Side: Mountains grow steeper and more rugged, with tall peaks, hanging valleys, dense woods and a cluster of buildings tucked against the slope; pine trees stand prominently in the foreground.
- The Far Shore: Distant ranges dissolve into mist, rendered with the palest of ink washes.

3. Style and Seals
The aged paper ground is warm beige. The brushwork is extraordinarily varied — long hemp-fibre texture strokes, alum-dot foliage, wet and dry contrasting washes — typical of the mature style of one of the Four Masters of the Yuan dynasty. Red collector seals appear along the margins, including imperial seals of the Qing dynasty (e.g. the Qianlong imperial reviewing seal).

Overall Impression
The work embodies the literati ideal: painting as the trace of a cultivated spirit wandering through familiar hills and water. Its serenity, economy of means and poetic self-effacement have made it one of the most beloved monuments of Chinese landscape painting, famously damaged by fire in the Qing era and hence transmitted in two separated sections.
